# Question
In our interview product, a language model scores each answer, but the final hiring score and the recommendation are calculated in plain code, not by the model. Why would you design it that way?

# Answer
Anything that must be consistent and explainable should not depend on the model. Computing the overall score as a plain average and deriving the recommendation from fixed thresholds makes the outcome deterministic, auditable and easy to explain to a hiring manager. Models are unreliable at arithmetic and can drift between runs. It also limits the damage of prompt injection: a candidate who talks the model into something still cannot change the thresholds or the formula. The model is used for what it is good at, judging the content of an answer, and code does the rest.

# Context
Comes from `src/evaluation/evaluator.py`. `judge()` asks Gemini for a structured judgement per question; `finalize()` then computes `overall_score` as the mean, maps it to a recommendation with `STRONG_HIRE_MIN` and `HIRE_MIN`, and forces unanswered questions to zero.

# Follow-up
## Question
The evaluator also checks every quote the model cites against the transcript. What problem does that solve, and where does that check fall short?

## Answer
It catches fabricated evidence: models sometimes invent or paraphrase quotes, and a hiring report must only cite things the candidate really said. Quotes that are not found are removed and the report is flagged for human review. The limits: it only proves the words were said, not that they support the score, so a real quote can still be misread or taken out of context. Exact matching can also reject a genuine quote if the model fixes a transcription error or paraphrases slightly.

## Context
In `finalize()`, quotes are normalised (lower case, punctuation stripped) and matched as substrings of everything the candidate said; dropped quotes add an entry to `review_reasons`.
